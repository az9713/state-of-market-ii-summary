The investment payoff · a two-minute read

Follow the cash.
Find the constraint.
Respect the price.

AI activity is expanding rapidly. Your return depends on which business retains the cash, whether it can deliver, and how much success its share price already assumes.

These are deductions from the deck, not a current buy list. Use them to decide what deserves further research.

01

Where does the cash land?

Hyperscalers are spending; suppliers are collecting. But several AI holdings can depend on the same customer budget. Revenue, tokens and private valuations each tell you less than retained cash.

Your move: trace the final payer and compare gross profit, free cash flow and financing needs. For private funds, ask how much cash has actually been distributed.

Evidence: the shared spending risk · Private marks versus cash
02

What actually limits growth?

The scarce input may be power, grid access or integration into customer workflows. A delivered GPU is not necessarily usable capacity; an AI deployment is not necessarily a profitable workflow.

Your move: look for a business that removes a binding constraint and can keep part of the value it creates. Verify delivery capacity, customer outcomes and pricing power.

Evidence: power and lead times · Adoption depth
03

What success is already priced in?

A great industry can contain expensive stocks. A low chip P/E can use peak earnings; a cheap software multiple can anticipate lost customers. A premium security business may already price in its advantages.

Your move: value sustainable cash flows under a slower-growth scenario. Buy only when your evidence supports expectations the price does not already require.

Evidence: the chip earnings denominator · Software durability

The two tensions become one test: can supply and demand adjust?

Electricity

More demand can spread existing grid costs across more customers. It can also overwhelm capacity and require expensive expansion.

Ask: can this location deliver new power, on time, at an economic cost?

Employment

AI can reduce labor per task while expanding the number of tasks. More hiring and greater efficiency can therefore coexist.

Ask: does the customer expand output, cut costs, or both—and does the vendor earn from that result?

What would change the conclusion?

  • Capex outruns paying demand: cash collections and gross profit fail to catch up with spending.
  • Scarcity eases faster than expected: utilization or rental pricing falls as new capacity arrives.
  • Adoption fails to deepen: trials grow but retention, workflow penetration and repeatable outcomes stall.
  • Growth fails to reach owners: debt service, replacement capex, dilution or fees absorb the gains.

Page convention: references use 1-based PDF page numbers, which agree with the visible slide numbers on the content pages. The central investment conclusion

The deck describes a transition from an economy dominated by capital-light technology profits to one where those profits finance an increasingly capital-intensive supply chain. At the same time, AI applications are becoming cheaper and spreading, but their adoption and economic benefits remain extremely uneven. The strongest investment question is who can retain cash when compute becomes abundant, rather than who currently has the fastest revenue growth.

Five conclusions carry the greatest weight: apparent diversification across AI suppliers can conceal common dependence on hyperscaler spending; power and grid lead times can become more binding than GPU supply; token growth is not interchangeable with paid revenue or compute work; software valuations reflect both slower growth and uncertain future defensibility; and private-market value creation is accompanied by substantial concentration and limited cash realization.

1. AI can be economically real while individual investments remain overpriced

Evidence combined. Technology contributes a disproportionately large share of earnings and margin expansion. The deck decomposes 2026 stock returns into earnings growth and multiple changes, showing that technology's gains coexist with falling multiples. Yet retail leverage and momentum exposure have also increased.

Cross-chart inference. “Real earnings” and “speculative positioning” can coexist. Fundamental justification for a sector does not establish a margin of safety for every constituent. Earnings forecasts can themselves contain peak margins, and a lower forward multiple can still imply expensive normalized earnings.

Investment implication. Underwrite through-cycle earnings and financing resilience, then consider valuation. Do not use the sector's aggregate earnings contribution as a substitute for company analysis. Leveraged ownership can amplify a drawdown even when the long-term business thesis remains intact.

Graphs: p.7, “Tech Contributed ~76% Of Earnings Growth”; p.8, “Profit Margin: Tech vs. The Rest (It's No Contest)”; p.11, “Returns Are Driven By Profits, Not Price” and “Tech P/E Multiples Are Well-Below Long-Term Averages”; p.13, “Momentum On The Way Up (And On The Way Down)” and “Retail Goes Long Levered ETFs.” Confidence: high for the coexistence; medium for any valuation conclusion.

2. Diversifying across AI industries may still concentrate one underlying spending risk

Evidence combined. Hyperscaler capital spending climbs as their free cash flow falls. Semiconductor free cash flow rises sharply. The rest of tech outperforms the largest hyperscalers. The supply-chain diagram allocates the same investment stream across chips, networking, power, cooling and construction.

Cross-chart inference. These businesses can have different sector labels but share the same customer budget. Owning a chipmaker, an optical supplier, a cooling company and a data-center builder is partly repeated exposure to hyperscaler investment decisions. Their revenue recognition and backlog timing differ, which can obscure the linkage.

Investment implication. Map portfolio exposure by final payer, not just sector. Stress-test a reduction or delay in hyperscaler capex across the entire chain, allowing different lags for equipment shipments and construction.

Graphs: p.12, “Rest Of Tech, Not Mag 7, Has Carried The Year”; p.17, “Capex Is Eating Cashflows . . . For Now”; p.43, “Hyperscaler FCF Has Become Semiconductor FCF”; p.44, “Following The AI Capex Money”; p.53, “AI Supply Chain (Especially Memory) Win The Year.” Confidence: high for the common economic driver; security-level sensitivities require separate work.

3. The buildout is evolving from a cash-financed boom into a credit-sensitive cycle

Evidence combined. Capex forecasts repeatedly move upward, free cash flow approaches or falls below zero for the aggregated hyperscalers, and on- and off-balance-sheet borrowing grows. The deck forecasts a substantial free-cash-flow recovery later in the decade.

Cross-chart inference. Financing conditions matter more as internal funding becomes insufficient. A spending slowdown could be triggered by financing discipline before end-user AI demand actually declines. Strong customers do not make every project or borrower equally creditworthy.

Investment implication. Examine debt maturities, lease commitments, guarantees, customer concentration, refinancing exposure and the allocation of residual-asset risk. The relevant question for a neocloud is whether contracted cash receipts cover debt service and asset replacement under adverse utilization and pricing.

Graphs: p.16, “Hyperscaler Capex Keeps Exceeding Expectations”; p.17, “Capex Is Eating Cashflows . . . For Now”; p.18, “Borrowing Bigly For The Build Out” and “AI Borrowing Is Longer Term Commitment”; p.24, “Hyperscaler FCF Back With A Vengeance(?)”; p.25, “Neoclouds Are Growing Quickly.” Confidence: high for increasing financing dependence; the FCF rebound is a forecast.

4. Existing hyperscaler profitability does not prove the return on the next dollar of AI capex

Evidence combined. The return-on-capital graph shows strong historical company-level returns. Meanwhile, investment intensity rises dramatically and cloud backlogs increase. Future free cash flow is expected to recover.

Cross-chart inference. Historical returns include established advertising, software and cloud franchises. Those returns are not an estimate of incremental returns from today's AI projects. Backlog is a forward demand signal, but conversion timing, margins, cancellation provisions and required investment determine its equity value.

Investment implication. Separate legacy franchise returns from incremental AI returns. Ask for revenue generated per dollar of new infrastructure, utilization, depreciation, replacement spending and contribution margin after energy and inference costs.

Graphs: p.19, “Hyperscaler ROIC Is Above Their Cost Of Borrowing”; p.17, “Capex Is Eating Cashflows . . . For Now”; p.24, “Cloud Revenue Backlog Piling Up” and “Hyperscaler FCF Back With A Vengeance(?).” Source issue: p.19's slide heading says ROE, while its graph says ROIC. Its legend also places the line labelled cost of debt near 10% and WACC near 4%; that ordering deserves verification against the underlying BofA data. I have not silently relabelled it. Confidence: high for the distinction between historical and marginal returns.

5. Scarcity may migrate from chips to the ability to energize them

Evidence combined. The lead-time matrix places GPU systems at roughly 20–30 weeks, compared with 104–260 weeks for transformers and 260–420 weeks for gas turbines. Power, networking and cooling account for substantial portions of the illustrative capex budget. Electricity demand also has non-AI sources.

Cross-chart inference. Delivering accelerators is not the same as delivering usable capacity. A binding constraint elsewhere can delay revenue across the entire project. Long-lived electrical infrastructure also has a different replacement cycle from compute hardware.

Investment implication. Research businesses with proven capacity to deliver switchgear, transformers, power distribution, interconnection and cooling. Distinguish component shortages from permits and grid-access delays. Long lead times support demand visibility but do not eliminate cancellation or overexpansion risk.

Graphs: p.44, “Following The AI Capex Money”; p.47, “Growing Electricity Demand Is More Than Data Centers” and “Utilities Capex To Exceed $1T By 2030”; p.55, “Buildout Backlog: At Least The GPUs Are Timely.” The p.47 $1T headline refers to spending over a period, not $1T of annual spending: the annual bars are roughly $200–280B in the forecast years. Confidence: high for the relative constraints reported; lead-time ranges are estimates and category-dependent.

6. The broader electrification thesis offers a route to reduce pure AI-budget dependence

Evidence combined. Infrastructure funding needs extend beyond data centers; electricity demand includes EVs, buildings, industry and cooling. Manufacturing recovery is uneven, with electrical equipment stronger than several other categories. Defense orders also rise.

Cross-chart inference. The “atoms” opportunity is broader than the AI supply chain, but not all industrial businesses participate equally. Suppliers serving several end markets may have more resilient demand than companies dependent on one compute customer.

Investment implication. Favor analysis of customer mix and product bottlenecks over indiscriminate industrial exposure. Seek evidence that non-AI orders can sustain returns if data-center demand disappoints. Global investment needs are not equivalent to funded contracts or addressable revenue for a particular company.

Graphs: p.45, “Global Infrastructure Needs Are Massive”; p.46, “U.S. Factory Activity In Expansion Mode” and “Manufacturing Output Is Running Ahead Of Capacity”; p.47, “Growing Electricity Demand Is More Than Data Centers”; p.51, “Defense Orders and Production Are On The March.” Confidence: medium-high for the diversification mechanism; funding and execution remain unresolved.

7. A low semiconductor P/E may be a warning about the earnings denominator

Evidence combined. Memory prices rise sharply, semiconductor returns exceed broad market returns, and the deck's forward-growth-versus-P/E scatter places several large chipmakers at apparently modest multiples. Another chart describes this as a narrow chip cycle rather than broad manufacturing strength.

Cross-chart inference. Rapid price inflation can inflate near-term margins and consensus EPS. Valuing a cyclical producer on those profits makes it look cheapest when earnings are closest to a peak. Conversely, persistent structural shortages could make the discount excessive. The deck establishes the debate rather than resolves it.

Investment implication. Model normalized pricing, supply additions, customer inventory and product mix. Separate commodity NAND/DRAM exposure from differentiated HBM, packaging and other products. HBM growth does not make every memory category equally durable.

Graphs: p.53, “NAND and DRAM Memory Prices To The Moon” and “AI Supply Chain (Especially Memory) Win The Year”; p.54, “A Narrow Chip Cycle: Earnings Are Growing More Quickly Than Broader Manufacturing Activity” and “Big Discounts on Big Chipmakers?”; p.55, “Buildout Backlog: At Least The GPUs Are Timely.” Confidence: high for the valuation hazard; medium for the eventual cycle outcome.

8. Rising token volume is an incomplete investment KPI

Evidence combined. Agents consume rapidly increasing tokens; 86% of their measured token volume in the caching chart is cached prompt traffic. Frontier labs capture about 70% of spending on roughly 35% of tokens. Model routing can reduce task costs while maintaining quality on a particular benchmark.

Cross-chart inference. Tokens are economically heterogeneous. Cached inputs, fresh inputs, reasoning and generated output have different prices and computational requirements. A large rise in counted tokens need not generate a proportional rise in revenue, FLOPs or electricity demand.

Investment implication. Track spend, useful completed tasks, inference gross profit, cache mix and cost per successful task alongside token volume. When evaluating infrastructure capacity, reconcile usage with accelerator-hours and power consumption. The deck's traffic samples are not the entire AI market.

Graphs: p.35, “Agents Are Using Far More Tokens Than People” and “Agents Are Caching In”; p.36, “Frontier Labs Capture ~70% of the Spend, On 35% Of The Tokens (With Tokens Up 600x Over 2 Years)”; p.78, “Token Demand ~27x YoY, And Doubling ~Monthly”; p.80, “Databricks ‘Smart Router’ Seeks The Efficient Frontier.” Confidence: high for the measurement distinction.

9. Jevons-style demand growth supports capacity expansion, but does not guarantee supplier margins

Evidence combined. Intelligence prices fall quickly while GPU rental prices hold up or rise for several products. Older models retain spending share, routing reduces costs, and agent use expands.

Cross-chart inference. Efficiency can unlock enough new demand to offset reduced resource use per task. However, volume expansion can coexist with competition and declining revenue per unit. GPU scarcity today does not establish the long-run economics after capacity catches up.

Investment implication. Distinguish task-demand elasticity, compute-demand elasticity and supplier pricing power. A provider with lower cost and broad distribution may gain even if industry-wide pricing falls. Measure utilization and realized pricing rather than extrapolate a token-growth curve indefinitely.

Graphs: p.34, “The Price of Intelligence Is Falling Quickly” and “Jevons: Compute Prices Rise As Token Prices Fall”; p.37, “Price/Token Is Falling, Driven By Proprietary Models,” “While Older Models Learn New Tricks,” and “Token Demand Drives GPU Rental Prices (Even For Older GPUs)”; p.80, “Databricks ‘Smart Router’ Seeks The Efficient Frontier.” External check: Databricks' original post reports internal routing cost savings above 30% at roughly comparable quality, while also describing growing AI expenditure as a cost-management problem. This supports the task-economics interpretation, not an unlimited-margin thesis. Confidence: high for the observed coexistence; medium for persistence.

10. Adoption breadth is much less informative than adoption depth

Evidence combined. In the deck, 69% of S&P 500 firms point to a live deployment, 29% quantify a result, and only 2% disclose a tracked metric. Power users spend far more than the field. Plugin and skill adoption is much higher among frontier firms and OpenAI's own users than among typical firms.

Cross-chart inference. “Using AI” is not a uniform economic state. Organizational integration and repeatable workflows appear to differentiate intensive users from casual adopters. Future growth may come as much from greater use by existing customers as from new logos.

Investment implication. Ask for cohort expansion, task completion, workflow penetration and repeated outcome measurement. Providers selling operational integration, permissions, data access, evaluation and observability may participate in adoption deepening. Yet low disclosure does not prove low ROI: some firms may have benefits they do not report publicly.

Graphs: p.27, “Companies With Quantifiable AI Impact Rising” and “69% of the S&P 500 Point To A Live AI deployment. Only 2% Disclose A Metric They Track Over Time”; p.33, “AI Power Users Pulling Away From The Field”; p.79, “AI Power Users Are Using AI Power Tools.” Confidence: high for uneven usage; medium for the causal role of integration.

11. AI may create more software demand even as it lowers the cost of making software

Evidence combined. Code creation and repositories increase; high-intensity AI adopters show better entry-level hiring trajectories than low-intensity adopters in one dataset; technical employment exceeds a modeled trend; security/observability outperforms other software categories.

Cross-chart inference. Cheaper development can expand the stock of software needing deployment, access control, monitoring and maintenance. The complementary demand can grow even if some existing software licenses lose value.

Investment implication. Research businesses paid for managing growing complexity and exposure. Distinguish increased code volume from useful production applications, and hiring correlations from causal labor-market effects. More repositories alone are not evidence of more monetizable workloads.

Graphs: p.30, “AI's Code-splosion” and “Gen Z Entrepreneurship Is Growing”; p.31, “AI is Taking Making Entry-Level Jobs?” and “Above Trend Hiring For Technical Roles”; p.58, “Cyber Is Still The Runaway Software Winner.” Confidence: medium; this is a complementary-demand hypothesis supported by several observations.

12. The software selloff contains both a maturity repricing and a terminal-value debate

Evidence combined. Public software growth has slowed but stabilized; operating leverage generally improves; profitable companies become more common. Lower-growth software multiples compress more than high-growth multiples. Stripe's sampled SaaS payment data shows a recent acceleration, especially in younger companies. The newspaper analogy warns that share prices can decline well before earnings collapse.

Cross-chart inference. Improving current results do not resolve long-term disruption risk. At the same time, broad “software is dying” claims conflate mature incumbents with fast-growing new applications. A sector can grow while particular vendors lose their moats.

Investment implication. Separate observed growth/margin maturation from plausible future displacement. Evaluate control of workflow, data rights, distribution and switching costs. A low multiple is attractive only if the cash flows are durable after considering seat compression, bundling and cheaper substitutes.

Graphs: p.59, “High Growth Software Multiples Have Normalized. Slower Growth, Not So Much”; p.60, “Software Revenue Growth Has Slowed, But Stabilized” and “Operating Leverage For Software Cos Stable Or Improving”; p.61, “~75% Of Software Is Profitable Now, But Only ~30% Is Growing 20%+”; p.62, “Newspaper Stocks Sold-Off 5 Years Before Earnings Collapsed”; p.83, “Narrative Violation: SaaS Accelerated Into The ‘SaaSpocalypse’.” Confidence: high for the coexistence; future displacement is unresolved.

13. Cybersecurity's favorable demand position does not automatically make it the cheapest opportunity

Evidence combined. Cyber/observability outperforms vertical and horizontal SaaS, while receiving premium revenue multiples. Code activity rises rapidly and AI users deploy more sophisticated tooling. Vertical and horizontal software have experienced different degrees of repricing.

Cross-chart inference. The market already recognizes much of the complementary security demand. Superior expected growth can be offset by a higher entry price. Conversely, a deeply discounted software company may be cheap for a valid defensibility reason.

Investment implication. Compare durable growth and cash generation to the expectations embedded in each valuation. Require a company-specific reason for either paying the cyber premium or accepting incumbent software disruption risk.

Graphs: p.58, “Cyber Is Still The Runaway Software Winner” and “Of All The Software, Cyber Gets the Premium Multiple”; p.59, “Multiple Compression: Some Tech Fared Better Than Others”; p.30, “AI's Code-splosion”; p.79, “AI Power Users Are Using AI Power Tools.” Confidence: high for the relative pricing; returns require current valuations and company-level underwriting.

14. Hardware leadership may precede application leadership, but the timing is not established

Evidence combined. The mobile-internet analogy places semiconductors before infrastructure and software. The current cycle has a similar early hardware lead. Application and lab revenues grow quickly, while forecasts eventually restore hyperscaler free cash flow. Capital-light businesses retain higher projected ROE than capital-intensive businesses.

Cross-chart inference. The present winner and the eventual winner need not be the same. Returns could migrate from scarce capacity to applications and distribution as infrastructure becomes more available. But a historical sequence is an analogy, not a trading clock.

Investment implication. Maintain a separate watchlist of firms converting AI into recurring customer outcomes. Require evidence of profitable monetization and retention before assuming an application wave will take over. Do not interpret hardware outperformance as proof that capital intensity is permanently superior.

Graphs: p.41, “First Semis, Then Software” and “Semis Then Software, Take Two (AI Edition)”; p.23, “Unprecedented Revenue Ramp”; p.52, “Asset-Heavy Flips-the-Field on Asset-Light”; p.63, “Great ROE Expectations For Capital Light Business”; p.24, “Hyperscaler FCF Back With A Vengeance(?).” Confidence: medium; timing and eventual winners are speculative.

15. Consumer adoption presents a distribution opportunity, not merely a subscriptions opportunity

Evidence combined. Paid household adoption is still low in the dated survey, subscription spending rises, and user retention curves improve. Search referral traffic declines while measured AI referrals remain small. Meta's Muse quickly gains download share in the displayed short launch window. Company anecdotes describe higher shopping conversion or spending among AI users.

Cross-chart inference. AI can monetize through subscription, commerce, advertising or existing platforms. The economic value may appear in conversion and distribution before AI referral traffic becomes large. Download gains, however, do not establish enduring usage or profits.

Investment implication. Evaluate distribution, acquisition cost, cohort retention and monetization together. For advertising and commerce incumbents, test whether AI improves unit economics enough to offset infrastructure expense and interface disruption. Shopping-user comparisons can reflect selection effects.

Graphs: p.38, “More and More Households are Paying for AI,” “Steep Growth With Retention Curves That Smile,” and “Households are Spending More on AI”; p.39, “Search Is Less of a Source”; p.40, “Muse Has Entered The Chat”; p.28, “Anecdata Around AI ROI Abound” (slide with company quotations). Confidence: medium-high for expanding channels; medium for causal monetization benefits.

16. Private-for-longer changes how public investors access innovation

Evidence combined. Large private companies rival listed mega-caps, aggregate unicorn valuations exceed the Russell 2000 in the deck, and companies reach public markets later. New unicorns get younger even while the full unicorn population ages. The growth distribution differs sharply between newly funded and typical startups.

Cross-chart inference. Some early company growth is occurring before public investors can participate. But private access at a late-stage valuation is not equivalent to access to early-stage returns. Faster-growing new entrants coexist with an aging pool of legacy unicorns.

Investment implication. Do not assume a public small-cap allocation captures the newest technology cohort. Equally, do not pay an illiquidity premium simply to access a famous private name. Examine entry price, preference structure, dilution, exit prospects and the actual share class.

Graphs: p.66, “$3-4 Trillion in Value Accruing On The Private Side” and “Five Largest Private Companies Are Larger Than the Past Decade of Tech IPOs Combined”; p.67, “Private-For-Longer Means Value Accrues On The Private Side” and “Unicorns Have Lapped Small Caps”; p.73, “Startups Trade Growth For Profitability (Except The Ones Raising)”; p.75, “A New Breed Of Unicorn: New Unicorns Get Younger”; p.76, “Post-AI Startups On A Different Growth Curve.” Confidence: high for access differences; valuation comparisons are based on latest private marks, not common-date liquid prices.

17. Private-market wealth creation and spendable investor returns are very different quantities

Evidence combined. Exit value is extremely concentrated, secondary demand is strong, and leading VC portfolios appreciate faster than the selected public comparison. Yet the vintage table reports little cash distribution for many funds despite positive reported IRR or TVPI.

Cross-chart inference. A valuation mark, an IPO company's total equity value and cash returned to a fund investor are not interchangeable. High exit-value statistics can be dominated by a few transactions without implying broad liquidity. Recent vintages also have had less time to distribute cash, so comparing their DPI with old vintages requires care.

Investment implication. For private funds prioritize net cash flows, DPI, valuation policies, fees, unfunded commitments and liquidity planning alongside TVPI and IRR. Historical top-decile performance is not a way to identify tomorrow's top decile without an independent selection process.

Graphs: p.65, “A New High Watermark For IPOs”; p.68, “Top 1% Of Exits Account For 84% Of Exit Value, And Top 10% Account for 94%”; p.69, “No Discounts In The Secondary Market”; p.70, “Top VC Portfolios Have Appreciated A Lot More Than The Top 10 Public Cos”; p.71, “VC Dispersion Has Never Been Wider” and “It Pays To Be Top Decile.” Concrete check: p.71's 2017-vintage median TVPI is 1.64x, but median DPI is only 0.31x; the 2021 median TVPI is 1.04x and DPI 0.00x. Confidence: high for the cash-versus-mark distinction.

18. AI concentration appears at several levels, but the denominators differ

Evidence combined. Power users dominate spending, frontier labs capture disproportionate spending per token, 86% of US VC deal value is AI-related in the displayed 2026 bar, and exit values are dominated by the upper tail. Top managers and selected private firms also capture exceptional value.

Cross-chart inference. Aggregate expansion is compatible with weak outcomes for the median company or fund. A portfolio assembled from many AI names can still miss the few firms retaining economic rents. However, the different “top 1%” statistics refer to different populations; they cannot be combined into a single universal distribution.

Investment implication. Underwrite bottleneck control, distribution, differentiated products and repeatable customer expansion. Avoid treating the AI label as a moat. Broad funds and thematic baskets need scrutiny of holdings, concentration, fees and actual revenue exposure.

Graphs: p.33, “AI Power Usage: Top 1% Outspends The Top 10% By ~8X”; p.36, “Frontier Labs Capture ~70% of the Spend, On 35% Of The Tokens (With Tokens Up 600x Over 2 Years)”; p.68, “Top 1% Of Exits Account For 84% Of Exit Value, And Top 10% Account for 94%”; p.74, “Everything Is AI Computer”; p.75, “Unicorns: Mostly Profitable, Mostly Growing <20%”; p.76, “Top AI Apps Growing ~5x Off A Smaller Base And ~2.5x Off A Larger Base.” Confidence: high for unevenness within the displayed samples.

19. Inference revenue growth can look cheap without being economically comparable to software growth

Evidence combined. The private inference comparison shows roughly three-quarters of the selected public software firms' added ARR at about one-fifth of their equity value. GPU rentals remain firm, caching increases and routing lowers task costs. Neocloud growth is rapid.

Cross-chart inference. Added ARR is not gross profit, and usage-based infrastructure can have greater cost exposure than software. Comparing incremental ARR with total equity value also ignores the existing revenue base, retention, financing, dilution and mature franchise economics.

Investment implication. Compare enterprise value to normalized gross profit and free cash flow, not merely ARR growth. Check whether hardware is owned or leased, whether customers are concentrated, whether compute cost can be passed through, and whether differentiation survives cheaper capacity.

Graphs: p.82, “Inference Added ~3/4 The Revenue, at ~1/5 The EV”; p.25, “Neoclouds Are Growing Quickly”; p.26, “Reports Of Obsolescence Greatly Exaggerated: GPUs Getting More Valuable Over Time”; p.35, “Agents Are Caching In”; p.80, “Databricks ‘Smart Router’ Seeks The Efficient Frontier.” Source issue: p.82's graph title says EV, but its right-hand axis says equity value. Those are different financial quantities. Confidence: high for the comparability problem; no conclusion that private inference firms are cheap follows from this chart alone.

20. Physical AI creates opportunities in components and services, but cost reductions can disrupt incumbents

Evidence combined. Robots require substantial numbers of motors, bearings and reducers; robotaxi use grows; launch costs decline as payload expands and commercial ownership rises; defense spending and orders increase while new munitions promise lower unit costs.

Cross-chart inference. AI expands physical activity and shifts technology economics. More units do not necessarily translate into proportionately more revenue for incumbents if unit prices fall. Demand growth may favor efficient components, manufacturing and service businesses over firms relying on historical product pricing.

Investment implication. For robotics, analyze qualified suppliers and component content rather than distant robot-sales forecasts alone. For defense and space, distinguish demonstrated performance, funded orders, production capacity, unit costs and service revenue. A cheaper missile is not automatically equivalent in mission capability.

Graphs: p.48, “Cheaper Rocket Launches → More Payload” and “Outer Space Is A Place Of Business”; p.49, “We're Going To Need A Lot More Robots . . .” and “. . . 21. Deployment evidence is more valuable than demos, but a plotted benefit still needs causal scrutiny

Evidence combined. Company quotations describe shopping, ad, underwriting and cost benefits; localized AI voices are associated with improved leasing outcomes; Flock deployment studies report crime and recovery improvements; a healthcare chart associates higher ambient-AI adoption with lower mortality.

Cross-chart inference. Narrow integrations can produce measurable value even before general-purpose agents transform entire businesses. That favors examining real workflow economics. But these examples vary substantially in evidentiary strength, and portfolio-company selection can overrepresent success.

Investment implication. Require a repeatable outcome, identified payer, customer retention and defensible distribution. Inspect study design, baseline trends, selection, confidence intervals and whether the reported effect actually reaches the vendor's cash flow.

Graphs: p.28, “Anecdata Around AI ROI Abound” (slide); p.84, “Some evidence shows mortality falls in health systems adopting AI fastest”; p.85, “Improving The Tenant Experience With AI”; p.87, “Flock Reduced Auto-Theft, Increased Clearance Rates, And Shortened Auto-Recovery Times.” Important limit: p.84 shows overlapping error bars and supplies no identifiable study citation or sample-size information on the slide. It does not, by itself, establish that AI caused a mortality reduction. The p.87 comparison also needs its underlying research design before making a causal investment claim. Confidence: medium for the opportunity; low for causal interpretations unsupported by the displayed methods.

22. AI is not the only source of economic value in the deck

Evidence combined. Whatnot and collectible-card activity rise, with the chart comparing collectible prices to technology stocks. Separately, AI concentrates attention and spending, and cheaper digital creation increases the supply of reproducible content.

Cross-chart inference. Distribution, community and scarce goods can create value alongside AI. A platform monetizing transactions is a different exposure from owning the collectibles themselves. The chart does not demonstrate that AI caused demand for scarcity; that connection is a hypothesis, not an observed mechanism.

Investment implication. Do not let the AI narrative crowd out platform economics elsewhere. Evaluate take rate, repeat purchase, liquidity, fraud costs and acquisition expense. A selected collectible's outperformance over a short interval is not an investment case for collectibles as an asset class.

Graphs: p.88, “Collect Them All, While Supplies Last,” including “Pokémon Prices vs. Technology Stocks” and “Pokémon Sales and Whatnot WAU”; p.74, “Everything Is AI Computer”; p.38, “Steep Growth With Retention Curves That Smile.” Confidence: high for the platform-versus-asset distinction; low for the AI-to-scarcity causal hypothesis.

Two important tensions the full deck reveals

Electricity: past scale economies versus future scarcity. Page 21 cites historical evidence that data centers lowered average residential electricity rates; pp.47 and 55 show increasing load and long infrastructure lead times. These statements can both be true: spreading existing fixed costs is beneficial until new capacity and bottlenecks dominate marginal costs. The original research abstract explicitly cautions that future supply constraints could reverse the estimated historical effect. Its EPRI summary describes roughly 4% lower residential rates per doubling of data-center capacity. This is not a forecast that every community will have lower future bills. Graph reference: p.21, “The Largest Increases in Electricity Prices Came with the Largest Decreases In Demand,” combined with p.47's demand/capex graphs and p.55's lead-time matrix.

Labor: productivity versus expansion. Page 20 shows construction hiring and wage premiums; p.31 shows a favorable hiring association for intensive AI adopters; p.60 shows improving software efficiency. Productivity gains and more hiring can coexist when demand expands, but none of these charts isolates AI's net effect on total employment. Graph references: p.20, “Data Centers Pay Premium Wages” and “Data Centers Have Caused a Surge in Construction And Skilled Trades Hiring, And Demand Is Accelerating”; p.31, “Above Trend Hiring For Technical Roles”; p.60, “Operating Leverage For Software Cos Stable Or Improving.”

Evidence quality and reading traps

  • Forecasts versus outcomes: pp.15–17, 23–25, 47, 49, 54 and 63 include estimates, forecasts or forward metrics. Do not infer realized profitability from forecast recovery.
  • Counts and definitions: “Tech” is a custom classification (p.2). The global-largest-company comparison excludes China (p.6). Different software panels have different samples; Stripe's payment sample differs from listed software firms. Their growth rates should not be directly merged.
  • Private marks versus liquid prices: pp.66–70 compare latest private valuations or portfolio marks with public prices. They differ in liquidity, share terms, dates, fees and accounting.
  • Annualized revenue versus annual recognized revenue: the lab ramp on p.23 uses ARR, while another panel compares revenue additions and estimates. ARR must not be substituted for GAAP annual revenue or profit.
  • Residual values: p.26 explicitly infers GPU residual values from rental rates. These are not independently observed liquidation proceeds. Compute scarcity can sustain old GPUs temporarily without validating every depreciation schedule.
  • Sample concentration: OpenRouter traffic is a platform sample. Top AI apps are a selected winner cohort. Tender participants are not the entire private market. Top VC portfolios are selected leaders, not an investable average.
  • Misleading universality: p.69's “No Discounts” title accompanies a distribution whose lower percentiles remain negative. Median pricing near the last financing round does not mean every security trades without a discount.
  • Unresolved labels: p.19 mixes ROE and ROIC and has a cost-of-capital legend worth checking; p.82 mixes EV and equity value. The p.41 AI panel retains a mobile-internet subtitle, and p.49's automation graph appears to have an unrelated GPU legend. Conclusions rely on visible content and stated metrics, not a presumed correction of the labels.
  • Clinical causality: the p.84 chart is exploratory association with overlapping uncertainty bands and insufficient methods on the slide. It is not a clinical efficacy finding.
  • Future opportunity page: p.89 names consumer, robotics, autonomy, biology, health, enterprise diffusion and American dynamism, but provides no quantitative valuation evidence for those areas.
